# Value of time multipliers for walk time, wait time, service headway, displacement time, reliability and congested travel time: review and meta-analysis of worldwide evidence

## ABSTRACT

This study provides the most comprehensive review and meta-analysis of time multipliers that are fundamental to transport planning and appraisal. The attributes covered are: walk time, wait time, access/egress time, connection time, out-of-vehicle time and service headway, which are largely characteristics of public transport trips; travel time in various degrees of congestion and parking search time, which are features of car travel; displacement time; and a range of variables related to travel time variability. The evidence assembled covers 2872 multipliers drawn from 606 studies across 52 countries over the years 1963 to 2023.

It was found that many of the multipliers vary between Revealed and Stated Preference data, with the former almost always larger, and over time, indicating a reduction where an effect was discerned. Other influences were detected from mode used, journey purpose and distance. The estimated meta-model implies credible multipliers across a range of illustrative scenarios. It can be used to deliver multipliers for use in planning and appraisal where none exist or to benchmark existing or emerging evidence. When compared with official recommendations, the meta-model’s implied multipliers challenge the long-established multipliers of two for walk and wait time, supporting a reduction to 1.75 or 1.50 depending upon the emphasis placed on Revealed or Stated Preference data. The results support official headway and late time multiplier recommendations but suggest refinements for the reliability ratio and congested time multipliers along with new guidance for displacement time multipliers.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Objectives and context

Travel time is at the heart of transport planning, forecasting, policy and appraisal. The value of travel time savings (VTTS) is often cited as the single most important parameter in transport appraisal (Mackie et al., 2001; Mouter, 2020). But travel time is incredibly miscellaneous. It has long been recognised that there are many other types oftime associated with travel in addition to in-vehicle time (IVT). These can be differentiated according to: the effort involved, of which the most notable is walking time; the conditions of experienced time, such as waiting and congested traffic; the stage of the trip, covering accessing a station or vehicle or time spent interchanging; convenience related factors, namely service frequency and the ability to depart at the desired time; and the uncertainty surrounding travel time, represented by late arrivals or some measure of the spread of journey times. The theory (DeSerpa, 1971) underpinning VTTS contains a term relating to the marginal utility of travel time which will depend upon the type of time and travel conditions.

The central VTTS parameters used in transport planning and appraisal typically refer to IVT under average or ‘normal’ conditions, and it is IVT that has received most attention in valuation exercises and also, as cited by Wardman et al. (2025), in review studies and meta-analyses. The estimates of valuations of other types of time are often expressed relative to the value of IVT, as so-called time multipliers. These are ratios of the marginal utility of the attribute in question and the marginal utility ofIVT, and do not contain the marginal utility of money present in VTTS.

The objective of this paper is to provide the most extensive review and meta-analysis so far undertaken of a wide range of time multipliers. It builds upon previous studies (Wardman, 2004; Wardman, 2013) with much additional evidence and, for the first time, a worldwide scope.

A substantial body of evidence has long existed for time multipliers,1 which are used in conjunction with VTTS in transport appraisal to monetise different time impacts as well as to create generalised cost metrics used in transport modelling and planning. Whilst time multipliers are therefore a fundamental feature of transport appraisal, enabling practitioners to compare and jointly consider the disparate types of time that are relevant to a policy or scheme, we are aware of only one meta-analysis of a wide range of time multipliers (Wardman, 2013). This was an extension of a meta-analysis of walk, wait and headway multipliers reported in Wardman (2001a). Other significant studies include the International Transport Forum (2014) and Commonwealth ofAustralia (2021) reviews of numerous multipliers in the context of public transport and the Wardman et al. (2016) review of European time multiplier evidence across modes and many attributes. With the exception ofthe latter three studies, reviews of time multipliers tend to focus on a single or only a few time attributes, are not always international in scope, and are often based on limited data. Examples include walk and wait time (McKnight, 1982; Waters, 1992; Wardman, 2004; Australian Transport Council, 2006; Wallis et al., 2013), travel time variability (Tseng, 2008; Li et al., 2010; Carrion and Levinson, 20122; Wardman and Batley, 2014)3, headway (Wardman, 2001a; Wallis et al., 2013) and congested travel time (Wardman and Ibanez, 2012).

A meta-analysis of time multipliers has several attractions. Whilst direct evidence on values of IVT that can be used for appraisal exists in many countries and contexts, or else can be deduced from relevant meta-analyses and by using conventions on how values relate to the wage rate, evidence and conventions relating to other travel attributes is less widespread. A meta-analysis of time multipliers would allow monetary valuations of these attributes to be deduced from local monetary values ofIVT. Time multipliers can reasonably be expected to be more stable and more transferable across countries and contexts and over time than monetary values since variations in the marginal utility of money will cancel out when taking ratios of valuations and there is then no need to account for inflation, income growth and exchange rates. Even in appraisal contexts where evidence on valuations of time variables other than IVT is available, meta-analysis multiplier evidence could serve a useful benchmarking purpose. And although there exist multiplier conventions that can be used, a meta-analysis could query whether those which have been around for a long time, such as weighting walk and wait time at twice IVT, continue to apply. Moreover, multipliers are typically assumed to be constant, but this will not apply if either the monetary values ofthe non-IVT (numerator) variables or the monetary values ofIVT (denominator) vary across contexts, and meta-analysis is well suited to exploring the extent to which time multipliers vary according to methodological, temporal or contextual factors which would be novel and most welcome.

Since there have been several meta-analyses of monetary valuation evidence covering both IVT and a range of non-IVT variables, implicit time multiplier equations can therefore be obtained as the ratios of the elements of the meta-analysis equation relating to the non-IVTvariables relative to the element of the meta-analysis equation specific to IVT (Wardman, 2004; Abrantes and Wardman, 2011; Wardman et al. 2016). However, these equations relate to separate studies and other unaccounted for and confounding variations across studies could have a bearing, including but not limited to the adjustments needed for international data relating to exchange rates and purchasing power given that the equations relate to monetary valuations. The explicit analysis of within-study time multipliers and variations in them across studies, as reported here, provides a much more controlled context. In this respect, it is noted that the Wardman et al. (2016) study deduced time multipliers implied by the meta-analysis of a large amount of monetary valuation evidence for IVT and other time variables and found some quite large differences when comparison was made with the mean time multipliers obtained from the studies covered.

## 1.2. Scope

This meta-analysis covers estimated multipliers for the following 21 attributes, with the term used in referring to them given in brackets. Attributes 1–6 are predominantly connected with public transport, 7–10 cover car transport, and 11–21 relate to delay, scheduling and reliability with a degree of emphasis on public transport.

1. Walking time, as part of accessing or egressing a main mode and rare instances where it is separately identified as part of interchanging, but also as a main mode itself (**Walk**).

2. Waiting time at the start of a journey for a main mode and rare instances where waiting time was separately identified as part of interchanging or egressing (**Wait**). In some cases, which we did not record, the multiplier would relate to waiting time specified as half the service interval, usually for shorter distance trips with higher frequency services where this is a reasonable approximation, whilst in other cases it would have been estimated to reported or specified waiting time.

3. Out-of-vehicle time (**OVT**), which is here taken to be a combination of walking and waiting time accessing or egressing a main mode and instances where the walking and waiting time elements ofinterchange were also included as not separately identified.

4. Access/egress time, which is the time spent getting to and from modes including any waiting time, with its unique feature being that it can cover vehicular time and is not restricted to walking time (**AccEg**).

5. Connection time, which is the difference between the arrival time of one service and the departure time of the connecting service and is made up of both the walking time and the waiting time involved in changing between public transport services, and most usually the same mode (**Conn**).

6. Headway, which is the interval between timetabled public transport services (**Headway**).4 Where interchange is involved, the headway is effectively the maximum of the two legs. We assume RP studies allowed for this. And we assume that in SP exercises where there is an interchange and headway is specified the respondent takes the headway to relate to the end-to-end journey opportunities which seems appropriate. Very few bus journeys involve interchange and our feeling is most SP studies that value headway for other modes are based primarily on through services.

7. Time spent searching for a parking space (**Search**).

8. Car time spent in congested traffic conditions with no distinction of the degree of congestion (**Cong-nd**).5

9. Car time spent in moderately congested traffic conditions (**Cong-mod**)5.

10. Car time spent in heavily congested traffic conditions (**Cong-hvy**)5.

11. Displacement time, which denotes departing earlier or later than the preferred departure time6 but with no distinction as to whether the displacement time is earlier or later (**Disp-nd)**.

12. Displacement time that involves departing earlier than the preferred departure time (**Disp-earlier**).

13. Displacement time that involves departing later than the preferred departure time (**Disp-later**).

14. Mean late arrival time at the destination relative to timetable or expected arrival time that is estimated to a frequency distribution of, say, 5 or 10 late arrival times (**MeanLate-Distn**).

15. Mean late arrival time at the destination relative to timetable or expected arrival time that is estimated to a given amount oflate time for some proportion ofjourneys with the remainder being on time arrivals, such as 1 in 5 arrivals is 15 min late (**MeanLate-X/Y**).7

16. Early arrival at the destination relative to a preferred arrival time6, referred to as schedule delay early (**SDE**).

17. Late arrival at the destination relative to a preferred arrival time, referred to as schedule delay late (**SDL**).

18. The standard deviation of travel time, where the time multiplier is commonly referred to as the reliability ratio (**RR**).

According to Bates et al. (2001) and Fosgerau and Karlstrom (2010), under certain conditions there is an equivalence between the scheduling approach and the mean–variance (RR) approach. In other words, they give alternative representations of the same phe nomenon. Nonetheless, some studies, including but not limited to Small et al. (1999) and Significance et al. (2012), include both terms. Whilst this is presumably because there is a feeling that there are elements of unreliability that are not captured by schedule delay terms as well as the challenges specifying the latter, we would expect lower multipliers for the separate terms when they are each entered into the model. We additionally make the following distinctions for SDE, SDL and RR.

## 2. Summary of official time multiplier guidance

Section 1.1 pointed out that meta-analysis can serve a useful purpose in testing conventional wisdom and official appraisal guidance surrounding time multipliers. We here summarise the official multiplier recommendations provided by national or pannational administrations, building upon those reported in Mackie et al. (2014). These are relatively easily sourced and summarised, unlike the multipliers used in modelling studies, although we note that it is not uncommon that the latter adopt the multipliers of official guidance. In other words, official guidance not only steers appraisal practice but also modelling practice. We refrain here and elsewhere from consideration of and comparison with the results of specific studies since it is the purpose of this study to review the relevant empirical evidence in its entirety.

Table 1 presents the official multiplier recommendations of which we are aware. Only Australia provides recommended displacement time multipliers, of 0.33 for earlier and 0.50 for later, whilst it is noted that some countries use fixed penalties where interchange occurs instead of connection time multipliers. The countries that provide recommendations tend to be those that un dertake so-called national value of time studies to underpin that guidance and which, as is subsequently apparent in Table 4, provide most of the valuation evidence reviewed here.

There are 12 instances of Walk multiplier recommendations. The long-established convention of weighting Walk at twice IVT features for four administrations, with two each of 1.5 to 2 and of 1.5. The others tend to be in the range 1 to 1.5. Some administrations provide a Wait recommendation or a Headway recommendation but not both, on the grounds that different service headways have impacts in terms of waiting time. There are 12 Wait multiplier recommendations with five being 2, two in the range 1.5 to 2 and three of 1.5, with the remainder mainly between 1.1 and 1.4. There are recommendations for Conn multipliers in 6 cases and they centre around 1.5, although presumably Walk and/or Wait multipliers are used where there are no specific Conn multiplier recommendations. It seems that the customary weight of 2 for Walk and Wait is not quite as dominant as might be envisaged.

The recommended Headway multipliers tend to depend upon service interval, and to fall as services become less frequent. They tend to vary across a large range, being as high as 1 and as low as 0.2. However, these multipliers are not purely service headway effects, and at higher frequencies will represent waiting time more.

A development in recent years, presumably due to methodological advances and perhaps an increasingly unreliable world, is the amount of evidence relating to travel time variability, and 10 ofthe 15 administrations provide recommendations. There is a tendency for the RR to be used for unscheduled modes and late time (LT) for scheduled modes. The LT values at the destination tend to lie in the range 2.5 to 3.5 but, surprisingly, with larger valuations when there is lateness at the departure. The RR multiplier exhibits a large range from around 0.1 through to 1.4. Notably there is much more segmentation of the multipliers relating to travel time variability than the other multipliers.

Finally, a few administrations recommend congestion time multipliers. Two make no distinction between the degree of congestion and both recommend 1.5. The Norwegian values which distinguish moderate and heavy congestion tend to vary around 1.5 but the multipliers for light and heavy congestion in Singapore are both below this.

## 3. The Data

## 3.1. Data collection process

The multiplier evidence is drawn from studies that involve the modelling of travellers’ choices, covering Revealed Preference (RP) data based upon actual choice contexts or various forms of Stated Preference (SP) dealing with hypothetical choices.9

This study builds upon the meta-analysis reported in Wardman (2013) that examined 1389 time multipliers from 244 European studies and 18 countries covering the years between 1964 and 201110 and which were drawn from a dataset primarily collected to undertake meta-analysis of monetary valuations (Wardman et al., 2016). The additional multiplier data used in this study were collected as part of a World Bank funded study (Wardman et al., 2025) with an original aspect, compared to previous reviews, of extension to worldwide evidence.

Compared to Wardman (2013), there is here much more data. It is drawn from 606 (+148%) studies worldwide that yield 2872 (+107%) time multipliers covering 52 (+189%) countries with data collected between 1963 and 2023, and with coverage extending to additional attributes as set out in section 1.2. The full list of studies covered is provided in a supplementary file. As for the potential explanatory variables, the following information was collected for each multiplier observation, with further detail reported in Table 6.

• year of data collection and country;

• the number of observations in the estimated model11;

• whether the trips were urban, inter-urban or a mix of the two, along with an indicator of urban hierarchy;

• journey purpose;

• the modes used by the sample of travellers analysed and the modes to which the estimated values related;

• the type of data to which the valuation was estimated, which is either RP, SP or a combination of the two;

• the choice context examined and estimation method employed;

• where an SP exercise was used, the number of alternatives, attributes and choice situations offered, along with the means of presentation, experimental design procedure and means of customisation;

• the purpose of the study and whether the main aim was the valuation of the variable in question;

• the source of the evidence, covering academic journals, conference papers, published reports, and unpublished reports of various types;

• survey method, including on-mode, roadside or on-street interviews, interviews at stations/airports, hall tests, telephone contacts, home or workplace interviews, managed online panel, intercept, unsolicited mail-out, and use of mailing lists/social media; and

• whether the multiplier was obtained from a study that included a cost term orjust trade-offs between time-related variables.

A variety of means were used to identify the additional studies. These included: the use of search engines, such as Google Scholar, Scopus and Web of Science; the systematic search of numerous academic journals and accessible conference proceedings; and con tacting academics and consultants active in the field. The reference lists of included documents were examined (backward snow balling) and documents that subsequently cited identified research were followed up (forward snowballing).

The dataset was reviewed to identify and remove duplicate versions of the same study prior to proceeding to the main analysis. Results obtained from re-analysis of the same dataset were permissible if they provided new insights.

## 3.2. Key features of the assembled data

The source of the evidence is summarised in Table 2. Peer-reviewed journal articles are the most common source, and along with conference papers (some of which may have been published subsequently in journals) and published reports form the majority of the evidence. Nevertheless, given that unpublished reports (‘grey literature’) commissioned by governmental organisations, in the UK in particular, provided important and extensive evidence for our previous meta-analysis datasets, which have been used as a starting point for the present research, these still form a significant proportion ofthe values and studies, though in some cases they too will have been published subsequently.

Multiple observations were extracted per study either because the values relate to different attributes or else they are separate values for the same attribute but segmented by key influential variables such as distance, mode, journey purpose or data type and estimating the impacts ofthese variables is central to our investigation. Table 3 provides a summary. The average number of valuations per study is 4.7, with 20% of studies yielding only a single valuation and a majority (55%) providing three or less. Only 10% ofstudies delivered more than 10 multipliers, with 7 (1%) providing more than 25 and the maximum being 50.

The numbers of valuations and studies for each country are listed in Table 4, along with the World Bank income categorisation of high (H), upper-middle (UM) and lower-middle (LM). The ordering is according to the number of multiplier observations.

Given this study builds upon previous UK meta-analyses of monetary valuations of time-related attributes and a subsequent meta analysis of European evidence, it is hardly surprising that the UK has the largest proportion of valuations (36%) and ofstudies (30%).12

The economic appraisal of transport schemes is common in Western European countries, as is their ongoing commitment to na tional studies, and Denmark, Netherlands, Norway, Sweden and Switzerland provide 23% of valuations and 18% of studies. Australia, with its rich heritage of transport research, provides 9% of valuations and 9% of the studies whilst Singapore has conducted several national studies which have a strong public transport focus and yield a relatively large number of multipliers per study.

The perhaps surprisingly low number of valuations for the USA and the relatively few valuations per study for both the USA and Canada presumably stem from the dominance of the private car where the multipliers of interest here tend to be less relevant.

The UM countries provide 5% of valuations and 9% of studies whilst the respective figures are lower at 2% and 4% for the LM countries. The examination of valuations for attributes other than IVT would seem, up to now, to be a luxury of developed countries.

RP data provided 12% of the values, with 5% from joint RP-SP models and the remaining 83% from some form of SP exercise. 3% and 1% of the RP evidence is from UM and LM countries respectively, not greatly different to the 5% and 2% for the non-RP evidence. All countries provided non-RP evidence, three quarters of high income countries provided RP evidence but only a third of UM and LM countries did so. As to whether the estimation of the valuation in question was deemed to be the main purpose of the study, as opposed to incidental, these form around a third of all valuations.

Multipliers estimated to data collected up to 1980 provide only 2% of the valuations, with the years 1981–1990, 1991–2000, 2001–2010, and 2011 onwards respectively accounting for 11%, 27%, 35% and 25% of valuations. The corresponding pattern for the number of studies is little different at 4%, 11%, 25%, 32% and 28% respectively. Some increase over time would be anticipated but it might be argued that an element of saturation, more likely in developed countries, has been achieved on the grounds that the cu mulative knowledge base, more widespread official recommendations and the increased number of review studies reduce the need for further studies.

## 3.3. Summary multiplier valuations

We now turn to the valuations themselves. Table 5 reports descriptive statistics for the 21 attributes set out in section 1.2. It contains the mean value for each attribute, the standard error of the overall mean (SE) and associated 95% confidence interval (CI), the sample size (Obs) and number of studies from which the multipliers are drawn, the median and the sample skewness.13 The mean (Mean13), standard error (SE13) and sample size (Obs13) for the Wardman (2013) study upon which this study builds are provided in the final three columns. It is the purpose of the meta-analysis to explain variations in multipliers and hence no segmentations are here provided.

Starting with the sample sizes, Walk, Wait and Headway form by far the largest number of observations, consisting of 19%, 14% and 18% respectively. AccEg and the various forms of congested time contribute a further 10% and 12% respectively. There are relatively few observations for MeanLate-Distn (0.5%), Search (1.4%), and for SDE-withRR, SDL-withRR and RR-withSDE/L combined (1.5%).

The valuations have a positive skew for all but the smallest category ofSDE-withRR. The mean is greater than the median in all these cases except for Search. However, the degree of skewness is generally not great, being largest for AccEg and the various congestion multipliers and reflecting a few very large valuations, in excess of 3, for these variables.

The mean values for the multipliers for Walk, Wait, AccEg and Conn are remarkably similar, ranging between 1.65 and 1.72, with a slightly higher mean of 1.87 for OVT. The median values are lower, between 1.50 and 1.55, with 14% of the multipliers less than one and only 3% less than 0.8. These overall results contrast with the convention of valuing Walk and Wait at twice IVT.

The Headway multiplier averages 0.60 which seems credible. To interpret this figure in more detail, relationships can be formulated between Headway and Wait for high frequency services and between Headway and displacement time for less frequent services. For high frequency services, travellers can be expected to randomly arrive for their departure and then, given that on average waiting time would be half the service headway, the Headway multiplier would be half of the Wait multiplier. At 5 min service frequencies it can be assumed that everyone would be a random arriver and hence, given a Wait multiplier of $1 . 7 2 ,$ the implied Headway multiplier would be 0.86. This serves as an upper bound, and the mean Headway multiplier in Table 5 is consistent with it. As services become less frequent, the link between waiting time and headway is weakened as it becomes more likely that travellers plan their journeys according to scheduled departure times and simply arrive a number of minutes earlier, depending on various factors such as their risk aversion and the consequences of missing the service. The inconvenience ofservice headway is then represented by this ‘planning penalty’ plus the displacement time incurred.14 Assuming a uniform distribution of preferred departure times, the displacement time would be a quarter of the service headway. At a service headway of hourly the vast majority would be expected to plan their departure. If travellers arrive 5 min before departure and with a mean Wait value of 1.72, and given Disp-earlier, Disp-later and Disp-nd of around 0.6, a Headway multiplier of 0.29 is implied. This increases to 0.44 with arrival 10 min before departure. These figures serve as a lower bound.

$$
R R = \frac {\beta}{\alpha} \ln \left(1 + \frac {\gamma}{\beta}\right)\tag{1}
$$

Using the mean figures from Table 5 of 2.23 for γ and 0.96 for β, with α implicitly 1, yields an RR of 1.15 which corresponds reasonably well with the mean RR-withoutSDE/L of 0.93. Displacement time might in some circumstances be relatively highly valued, such as having to get up earlier in the morning for the journey to work or a later departure that would imply difficulties in getting to work on time. In the reverse direction, a fixed work end time would mean earlier departures are not possible and later departures would not be an attractive option, and hence each would imply relatively large valuations. But in the absence ofsuch constraints, displacement time might be regarded to be a relatively minor inconvenience and certainly valued less than IVT since departing X minutes later but with an X minute quickerjourney time and hence the same arrival time would be expected to be preferred to the prevailing situation. The general expectation is that displacement time is valued somewhat less than IVT and this is the case in the data collected, with average multipliers for Disp-earlier and Disp-later of 0.54 and 0.68 respectively and between the two for Disp-nd. The vast majority of the multipliers are less than 1.

As for comparison with the average values reported in Wardman (2013), the differences tend to be minor, with on average the new figures 0.08 smaller and only Headway $( \mathsf { t } = 3 . 3 )$ and SDL-withoutRR (t = 2.9) significantly different at the 5% level. The largest proportionate increase and reduction compared to the previous study are 31.2% and –23.9% for SDL-withoutRR and MeanLate-X/Y.

Whilst the vast majority of the multiplier evidence lies within a plausible range, there are inevitably some valuations that are somewhat outside what might be deemed plausible, most likely reflecting unreliable evidence and arising because the data collection focussed on monetary values with little attention paid to the credibility of the time multipliers. Given that the removal of ‘outlier’ observations can be contentious, the sensitivity of the mean values to different data exclusion criteria was investigated. We removed observations that were outside ±2.58 and ±1.96 standard deviations ofthe mean, representing 1% and 5% of observations, as well as observations deemed unreasonable compared to the conventional wisdom regarding the variable in question. Reassuringly, the mean values were not particularly sensitive to the removal of observations according to these criteria.

Although there are inevitably specific observations in our dataset which do not accord with theory, convention or reasonable expectation, in general the data assembled yields sensible mean values which are not very sensitive to the omission of what might be deemed outlier observations. Most multipliers lie within a relatively narrow and acceptable range. Hence we see no reason to omit data at the outset from the meta-analysis to which we now turn.

## 4. Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis is a specific form of empirical evidence review, here regression based, that seeks to explain variations across studies in parameters of interest, here travel time multipliers, in terms of a range of relevant explanatory factors. This multivariate approach has advantages over conventional literature reviews since it is more resilient to the confounding factors that can afflict simpler ap proaches, such as cross-tabulation. And the quantification of the impacts of influential variables provides methodological and policyoriented insights along with transferable context-specific estimates of multipliers which are useful for benchmarking alongside existing evidence and even more valuable where none exists.

The reported meta-models are multiplicative, since a MacKinnon, White, and Davidson (MWD) test (MacKinnon et al., 1983) found them to be statistically superior to an additive function whilst yielding generally more precise coefficient estimates. As such, the multiplier dependent variable is specified in logarithmic form.

We could carry out the regression analysis separately for each attribute. However, the approach adopted here is to pool data across multipliers in a single simultaneous estimation since this is more parsimonious than estimating separate models, given that some impacts will be common across multipliers, and to be able to account for study-level fixed-effects as many studies provide more than one multiplier. Our pooled model is specified as:

$$
\ln (M _ {i j s}) = \tau_ {J} + \sum_ {j ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {J - 1} \alpha_ {j ^ {\prime}} \cdot \delta_ {j, j ^ {\prime}} + \sum_ {j ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {J} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L _ {K} - 1} \beta_ {j ^ {\prime} l} ^ {k} \cdot \delta_ {l ^ {\prime}, l ^ {\prime}} ^ {k} \cdot \delta_ {j, j ^ {\prime}} + \sum_ {s ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {S - 1} \gamma_ {s ^ {\prime}} \cdot \delta_ {s, s ^ {\prime}} + \mu_ {i j s}\tag{2}
$$

The model explains variations in multipliers (M) relating to observation $i ,$ attribute j and study s. To allow for the simultaneous estimation over all attributes $j , J = W a l k$ is arbitrarily selected as the base attribute, represented by the constant term TJ. The term $\delta _ { j , j ^ { \prime } }$ is the standard Kronecker delta, having the value $1 \; { \mathrm { i f } }   j = j ^ { \prime } ,$ 0 otherwise.15 Parameters $\alpha _ { j }$ denote the proportionate effect of a particular attribute j’ relative to the base attribute J. We report these, alongside $\tau _ { J } ,$ as main effects.

In addition, a range of k factors which might influence the multipliers are considered via what we term segmentation effects (k factors that can only be drawn from the list of available explanatory variables detailed in 3.1). For each factor k (e.g. “data $\mathrm { t y p e ^ { \mathrm { { \textquotesingle } } } ) }$ , one level l (e.g. “data $\mathtt { t y p e } = \mathtt { S P }$ is arbitrarily selected as the base level (denoted as $L _ { K } )$ . An analogous Kronecker delta formulation $( \delta _ { l } ^ { k } , _ { l } )$ represents the inclusion ofthe segmentation effects, where $\beta _ { j l } ^ { k }$ denote the proportionate effect on a multiplierj of some level l of factor $k ,$ relative to the factor base level chosen $\left( \boldsymbol { L } _ { K } \right)$ .

Study-specific fixed-effects are captured by $\gamma _ { s } ,$ included in response to the suggestion of one of the reviewers. These might be useful in controlling for the impact of poor quality studies, discerning unidentified factors specific to a study over and above the random error term,16 and capturing any systematic differences across the multipliers of a given study. They are ‘catch-all’ terms covering all multipliers within a specific study. Finally, the error term $\mu _ { i j s }$ represents the net effect of unknown or unmeasurable influences on a multiplier observation in the estimated models.

A thorough and systematic process was followed to identify the impact of the numerous candidate segmentation variables on the multiplier valuations and to determine which of the $\beta_{jl}^{k}$ in Eq. (2) should be retained. Table 6 lists the k segmentation variables investigated along with their l levels and the number of observations in each category. Initially, each segmentation variable was examined in isolation; so, for example, we examined the impact ofthe journey purpose categories on each multiplier and took a view as to whether the estimated effects were considered to show potential to be taken forward into pooled segmentation models. The criteria for determining potential for further investigation was that at least one effect with significance at 10% or better could be recovered and that the variation had a possible explanation.

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">16 This might include publication bias which represents a tendency for studies to report models or to be accepted for publication where the key parameters accord with the ‘conventional wisdom’.</span></small>

Clearly, when the various separate segmentation effects deemed to merit further consideration are included simultaneously within a single model the precision with which they are estimated tends to fall. Starting with the model containing all the potential effects, we first removed those where the t ratio was less than 1 and re-estimated the model, which will tend to increase the precision of the remaining coefficient estimates. We erred on the side of caution and retained some where an effect was expected in the hope that subsequent models would find them more significant. The process was iteratively repeated, excluding coefficient estimates with t ratios less than 1.40, 1.64 and finally 1.96, but again exercising a degree of judgement on retaining some coefficients. In addition, some selective tests of hypotheses were undertaken as follows.

Firstly, we tested if the Walk multiplier varied according to whether the walking time was a main mode or was part of using other modes. There was very little variation according to this dimension.

Secondly, we tested whether the Walk multiplier was impacted by whether a country could ‘undisputably’ be categorised as being high temperature, on the grounds that then walking is more arduous.17 The incremental effect was not remotely significant.

Thirdly, a pattern that is sometimes found in empirical studies (Li et al., 2010; Hensher, 2015)18 is that the VTTS is lower if reliability is controlled for, the intuition being that without such control the VTTS also includes some unreliability effect. This would imply larger time multipliers. There are 175 observations of non-congestion multipliers in the data set where reliability was also included in the estimated model. The incremental effect for this segmentation was far from significant. We also split out 49 observations of congested multipliers since we might expect these to be lower where reliability is included, on the grounds they would otherwise discern a reliability effect. We found a significant effect with the congestion multipliers 22% larger when the reliability terms were also included but this is contrary to expectation and hence not retained.

Finally, we examined whether multipliers were different if they were obtained from studies which included a cost variable or where the trade-offs were solely between different types of time. This tests the hypothesis that biases in SP responses, particularly strategic bias, are most likely to focus on the cost term and therefore the impacts cancel out when estimating time multipliers. When the SP exercise does not contain cost, it is possible that the attention switches to travel time, which will then impact the time multipliers. No significant differences were detected.

As part of this investigation of segmentation effects, we also specified some composite terms, on the grounds of expected or actual similarity of effects or because of small sample sizes. These composite terms were:

• WWAOC which is Walk, Wait, AccEg, OVT and Conn combined;

• Late as an amalgamation of the three related variables of MeanLate-Distn, MeanLate-X/Y and SDL;

• Rely which is a combination of Late and RR;

• CongSearch which combines the car specific terms of Cong-nd, Cong-mod, Cong-hvy and Search;

• Disp which covers Disp-earlier, Disp-later and Disp-nd;

• WAC which is Walk, AccEg and Conn combined.

Table 7 reports the results of our preferred selection of meta-models. The coefficient estimates along with t ratios in parentheses (Coeff (t)) are reported as well as the percentage variation (%Effect). For the main effects, %Effect is specified relative to the base category of Walk, represented by the constant $\tau _ { J } ,$ and we provide for reference the implied multiplier for Walk after the adjustment of the constant, discussed in section 5, by adding half ofthe model’s mean square error (MSE) to it. For the segmentation effects, %Effect is relative to all non-included categories for the main effect multiplier being segmented. The notation for the segmentation effects specifies the main effect (j) that is segmented followed by the level (l) of variable k that forms the segmentation and is further described when we come to discuss the results (e.g., Wait\_RP gives the incremental effect of RP data type on the Wait multiplier).

Model I, which omits the fixed-effects, contains those coefficient estimates that were significant and correct sign at the usual 5% significance level as well as some which were not significant but deemed to merit retention. This model achieves an $\overline{R}^2$ goodness of fit only slightly larger than the 0.507 when no segmentation effects were specified. The correlations between estimated coefficients are encouragingly low; the only ones to exceed 0.5 in absolute terms were between Cong-nd and CongSearch\_NonMetropolis (-0.63), Dispearlier and Disp\_UseCar (-0.56) and Disp-later and Disp\_UseCar (-0.54). Looking beyond pairwise correlations, tolerance parameters determine ifmulticollinearity across the full set ofincluded variables is an issue. This involves regression of each independent variable on all other independent variables and $\mathbf { 1 - } \mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is each variable’s tolerance level. A tolerance level less than 0.2 is considered to indicate strong multicollinearity (O’Brien, 2007) and the lowest here were 0.19 for RR, 0.33 for Headway and 0.33 for CongSearch\_NonMetropolis, with the remainder greater than 0.4 and 75% greater than 0.65. The extent of multicollinearity is therefore very limited.

As for the main effects in Model I and indeed the other two reported models, the parameters relating to Wait, AccEg, OVT and Conn were all far from significantly different relative to the base of Walk, with Search a little lower. Although the overall implied multipliers for these variables will depend upon the segmentation effects, when we estimated simple models without any segmentation terms we recovered the same pattern of results. This similarity is not surprising, and indeed encouraging, given that Wait, AccEg, OVT and Conn might reasonably be regarded to have broadly similar levels of disutility to Walk. The same caveat applies regarding segmentation effects for the other attributes,19 but we note, as expected, lower multipliers for Headway, Disp-earlier, Disp-later and Disp-nd, larger multipliers for MeanLate-Distn, MeanLate-X/Y and SDL, and broadly sensible absolute and relative effects for Cong-nd, Cong-mod and Cong-hvy.

## 4.1. Mode effects

Information was collected on both the mode or modes used and the mode or modes being valued, as indicated in Table 6. It turned out that the only significant segmentations related to mode used.

Initial models indicated a tendency for the multipliers related to the largely public transport specific variables to be similarly lower for public transport users, although the effects for individual attributes were not significant. Given that public transport users will be more accustomed to these attributes, and self-selection means that they would value them less, we specified a single segmentation effect for Walk, Wait, AccEg, OVT and Conn and a significant effect was obtained for train, LRT and metro users (WWAOC\_Use-TrainLRTMetro) which indicated a multiplier 12.3% lower. Whilst monetary values of WWAOC might be conjectured to vary by more than this when compared with, say, car users, the higher values ofIVT for car users could have had a dampening effect on the variation.

Car users can be expected to dislike the inability to be able to travel when they want to relatively highly and hence to have relatively large headway values. An effect (Headway\_UseCar) was apparent in Model I, denoting 15.8% larger multipliers, but it was not sig nificant in Model III, and again the relatively large valuation of IVT for car users could have had a bearing.

The effect apparent on headway multipliers for LRT and Metro users (Headway\_UseLRTMetro) of 41.5% is one of the largest segmentation effects recovered. This is attributed to LRT and Metro users being accustomed to very high service frequency and that the attraction in using such systems is critically dependent upon it.

Although the number of headway multiplier observations for air travellers (Headway\_UseAir) is low, the highly significant effect is the largest in the model at -51.3%. Air travel is not characterised by high frequencies, and it is generally not expected, and it is reasonable that air users with their less frequent and highly planned journeys deem headway to be somewhat less important than other mode users.

Car users have 51.1% larger multipliers for displacement time (Disp\_UseCar) which presumably reflects their strong preference for being able to travel at their desired departure time.

A reliability effect was recovered for train and PT (bus, metro and LRT) users (Late\_UseTrainPT). This was based on the composite Late term since separate effects for MeanLate-Distn, MeanLate-X/Y and SDL were not significant but were indicating an effect was present. A 15.1% larger Late multiplier was apparent for these users. This may stem from a greater awareness oflate running for modes with schedules and lesser mitigation opportunities for train and PT users.

## 4.2. Journey purpose

It is expected, and commonly found, that the monetary valuation of IVT is largest for employers’ business (EB) trips, followed by commuting and leisure, all else equal. The effect on multipliers will depend upon the extent to which journey purpose impacts the monetary values ofthe non-IVT variables similarly. The Cost Savings Approach to valuing business travel time savings would contend that all forms of travel time are unproductive and savings in any of them would be converted into productive use to be valued at the marginal product of labour (Harrison, 1974). This approach is widely used in official recommendations around the world (Mackie et al., 2014) and assigns the same weight to Walk and Wait as to IVT for business travel.

Despite extensive searching for journey purpose effects, only two significant segmentation effects could be estimated. Business travellers have larger multipliers for displacement time (Disp\_EB), and it is understandable that they are more averse to switching from their desired departure time, whilst unsurprisingly commuters were found to have larger RR multipliers (RR\_Commute) although only 8.3% larger and we were unable to detect an equivalent effect for the other late time attributes. There was no support for the property of the Cost Savings Approach of valuing Walk, Wait and other non-IVT time variables the same as IVT for business travellers.

## 4.3. Distance and spatial effects

The analysis of distance and spatial effects covered: whether the journey was urban, inter-urban or was a mixture of the two; categories of urban hierarchy, essentially distinguishing between the size of urban locations; and World Bank country income classifications, which cover high (H), upper-middle (UM) and lower-middle (LM) income definitions. Limited country specific effects were also investigated.

The headway multiplier was found to be 29.1% lower for inter-urban trips (Headway\_Inter) which is not surprising since service frequency is much more of an issue for travellers making shorter and more regularly made journeys and high frequencies are not expected on longer distance journeys. It might also reflect the greater composition of wait time for shorter distance trips, where frequencies are higher, as discussed in section 3.3.

The composite Rely multiplier, defined above, was specified given relatively small sample sizes. It was found to be 19.8% lower than for urban trips (Rely\_Inter). Lateness is more common and less unacceptable for longer distance trips. Where no distinction was made between urban and inter-urban trips (Rely\_Mixture), the effect was essentially the same.

An argument sometimes found in the literature relates to a decrease in available time budget for longer trips, with less time remaining for other activities and making travel time savings for longer trips more valuable. This would not impact the time-related multipliers, but could contribute to these lower multipliers for Headway and Rely for inter-urban trips.

For urban trips, a further distinction was made according to whether the location was a major metropolis, including capital cities, a metropolitan area, an urban area, a rural area, or some mix of these area types. The key effects that emerged were that the congestion and search time multipliers were higher outside of the major metropolises. A combined term for these attributes (CongSearch\_NonMetropolis) indicated 13.9% higher valuations, which is interpreted as those in major metropolises being resigned to congestion and difficulties finding a parking space and hence are relatively less concerned about it. In addition, those who do not like congested travel conditions might be less likely to live in major metropolises.

The remaining statistically significant spatial effect relates to the World Bank income classification. Headway was found to have a 37.3% lower multiplier in UM and LM income countries (Headway\_UMLM). This lower valuation is not surprising. Whilst it is encouraging from the perspective of transferability that there are not widespread differences according to income classification, the limited number of observations for UM and LM income countries should be borne in mind.

As a final test, we explored whether the composite multipliers of WWAOC, Disp, CongSearch, and Rely were different for the UK, Australia and the Netherlands which are the three countries with very large numbers of multiplier observations. No significant effects were apparent.

## 4.4. Data type

Whilst there is primary and review evidence that monetary valuations differ according to whether they are estimated to RP or SP data (Wardman, 1988; Brownstone and Small, 2005; Shires and de Jong, 2009; Wardman et al., 2025), there has been little detailed analysis of whether time multipliers exhibit any such variation. Some prior cross-tabulations indicated that multipliers obtained from SP evidence were lower, and the differences were not trivial.

The dataset contains 342 (12%) RP observations and in 202 (59%) cases the journey components were obtained from network data and models generally estimated to secondary choice data with the remaining 140 (41%) based on reported data and models estimated to purpose collected survey data. Unsurprisingly, RP data is more common in earlier studies, providing 118 (33%) of observations up to 1990, and in the subsequent periods 1991–2000, 2001–2010 and after 2010 the respective figures are 65 (8%), 113 (10%) and 46 (7%). RP multipliers were available for all attributes except MeanLate-Distn but with only 2 available for SDE. As can be seen from Table 7, WWAOC terms form the most RP observations, with around three-quarters, made up of 70 for Walk, 78 for Wait, 59 for AccEg, 33 for OVT and 13 for Conn. CongSearch provided 8% with around 6% each for Headway, Disp and Rely. The main travel context covered was mode choice (68%), followed by route choice (14%), mode and destination choice (7%), departure time choice (6%) with other contexts and some combinations making up the remaining few, whilst 90% were drawn from developed countries.

Of the remaining observations, 133 were obtained fromjoint RP-SP models and 2397 from some form of SP model. SP evidence was treated as the base and, unsurprisingly given that SP data dominates RP-SP models but also perhaps because of the relatively few observations, there were no significant effects relating to RP-SP evidence.

An important issue to discuss here is the specification of reliability terms in RP models, given that it can be regarded to be more challenging than for SP models where the degree of lateness or spread of journey times is explicitly presented, and this might cast doubts on the validity of RP-based reliability multipliers. The RP reliability evidence here is drawn from 11 studies covering an even mix of route choice, mode choice and departure time choice studies, several of which paid particular attention to the measurement of reliability. Reliability was estimated by a range of methods, including the use of GPS, Smart data, traffic detectors and journey time surveys, along with respondents’ reported travel times and/or lateness. Even if these measures do not always accurately represent travellers’ perceptions, and the quality ofthe reliability estimates is better in some studies than others, these effects might offset in our meta-model.

Model I contains five segmentation terms for multipliers obtained from RP data. The effects for Walk, AccEg and Conn when estimated separately were indicating larger effects for RP data which were very similar but none were individually significant. They were therefore combined into a single term (WAC\_RP). The coefficient estimate is highly significant and indicates that these multipliers are 17.9% larger than when obtained from SP data. The Wait multiplier when RP based (Wait\_RP) is 33.5% larger whilst that for OVT (OVT\_RP) is similar to WAC\_RP at 20.9% larger. The impact on the Rely composite term (Rely\_RP) is not significant at the usual 5% level but its 16.3% effect is in line with other effects. The one exception to the positive RP effect is forHeadway (Headway\_RP) which is found to have a 22.9% lower value.

These are interesting and important findings. They could indicate that in SP studies respondents focus primarily on cost and IVT as the most important factors and the artificial and sometimes complex nature of the task results in other attributes not being fully considered which therefore leads to lower valuations than otherwise. Such an effect might be exacerbated by unrealistic attribute variation or poor explanation in SP studies, such as varying walk times to a fixed destination or varying public transport wait times when the traveller always turns up for the service X minutes before its scheduled departure. On the other hand, RP data requires assumptions about the trade-offs actually made by passengers, leading to less certainty regarding the reliability of inferred values, whilst RP multipliers might be larger due to possible confounding effects given that 33% of the multiplier evidence up to 1990 was from RP studies yet in subsequent periods the proportion is lower at around 10%.21 As for Headway, where the RP values are lower, a contributory factor could be poor specification ofservice headway in RP models22 or the use ofservice headway when in fact it is wait time that underpins choices.

## 4.5. Intertemporal effects

Whilst specific studies can provide evidence on how multipliers vary with mode, distance or even data type, they provide no insight into intertemporal effects, and this is where meta-analysis is particularly valuable.

Segmentations were undertaken according to when the data used in estimation was collected, with up to 1990 serving as the base and then segmentations specified for 1991–2000, 2001–2010, and after 2010, the exception being for RR where the base period was up to 2000. There was insufficient data to isolate post-covid variations.

Significantly different segmentation effects specified for the different time periods were not apparent and hence categories have been pooled according to similar effects and goodness of fit. Whilst an element ofjudgement is here involved, there is clearly support for several multipliers falling over time.

Model III finds the Walk multiplier (Walk\_1991+) to be 14.2% lower post 1990 with similar effects for Wait (Wait\_2001+) of a 15.3% reduction post 2000 and for the AccEg multiplier (AccEg\_1991+) which is 14.9% lower post 1990. The effects upon Conn and OVT, which were combined on the grounds of sample size, distinguish the years 2001–2010 (ConnOVT\_2001-10) and post 2010 (ConnOVT\_2011+) since, even though the former is significant only at the 10% level, there seems to be an effect at work here. The former indicates a 9.8% reduction and the latter a 14.7% reduction. As for RR, it was found to be 33.8% lower post 2010 (RR\_2011+), the largest intertemporal effect detected, although no significant effects could be discerned for the other late time variables.

There is evidence that the multipliers have fallen over time. The reason for the reductions in the Walk, Wait, Conn and OVT, and AccEg multipliers is likely that advances in the quality and quantity of mobile information and communications technology (ICT) means that more and better worthwhile activities can be undertaken while walking and waiting. However, the effects are not large, and this may be because these effects on the disutility of travel time also apply to the denominator term of IVT although not to the same extent. The impact on RR could be attributed to a greater acceptance of unreliability if there is a perception of an inevitably more unreliable world, as well as more and better real-time information which supports mitigation.

## 4.6. Data quality issues

A number of data quality issues were examined which merit pointing out. There were no significant impacts detected on the multipliers from: the use of advanced modelling techniques, such as mixed logit and hybrid choice models; efficient SP design procedures; whether the evidence was drawn from peer-reviewed academic journals, or indeed national studies undertaken to inform official recommendations; or the sample sizes of the models that provided the multipliers. But then why should poorer or higher quality studies yield systematically different multipliers across the data assembled? A random effect overall is by no means implausible and this is supported by the study-specific fixed-effects averaging close to zero. As for the precision of our estimates and quality, the removal of outlier observations did tend to increase precision but weighting the multiplier observations in Eq. (2) by the sample sizes of the models that provided those multipliers had very little impact.

## 5. Application: implied multipliers

Whilst meta-analysis can provide useful methodological insights, a primary motivation for this research as set out in section 1.1 is to summarise the evidence so as to be in a position to provide recommendations and also to make comparisons with official recom mendations, where they exist, and long-established conventions. Section 1.1 also specified that an attraction of meta-analysis is to quantify variations in multipliers, not least to aid transferability across contexts. Table 8 provides illustrative multipliers for a range of 46 scenarios, labelled as S1–S46, selected to cover combinations of variables upon which recommendations should be based and to demonstrate the range of potential variation across circumstances. The characteristics of the scenarios differ across multipliers given that the variables driving variations in their valuations differ. The implied multipliers for scenarios S1–S46 are based on Model III of Table 7.

Given that the estimated meta-model involves a logarithmic transformation, the unbiased predictor of the multiplier involves adding half the mean square error (MSE) to the estimate of the constant term (Miller, 1984). The revised constant used in deriving the implied multipliers is therefore 0.612. The fixed-effects have been ignored but, as already pointed out, their impact on the constant would be very slight.

The multipliers covered in Table 8 are the most important ones, in line with the official recommendations covered in Table 1. The scenarios are selected to cover the vast majority of the factors that drive the variations within each multiplier, with an emphasis on the range in which the implied multipliers might lie; the intertemporal effects and differences between RP and SP based multipliers are of particular interest. Hence the focus here is upon comparing the implied multipliers across a row rather than inspecting variations within columns.

The primary modes, prior to any combinations in our analysis, are car, bus, train, LRT/Metro, taxi, motorcycle, ferry and air. The multipliers were found to vary with mode used but there were no additional effects attributed to the mode to which the valuation relates.23 The scenarios in Table 8 distinguish all the modal effects identified for each multiplier and also all other segmentation effects and their base levels except for Rely\_Mixture, given that it is more appropriate to distinguish urban and inter-urban trips, and Headway\_UMLM, where the S17-S24 multipliers relate to high income countries and would be 37% lower otherwise.

Whilst the variations in multipliers arising from specific influential variables can be relatively small, there are some large but nonetheless credible variations when adding up over various effects as is apparent across the scenarios specified in Table 8. The implied multipliers for an attribute tend to vary, as would be expected, around the reference multipliers provided in the second column, which are the mean values across all observations as reported in Table 5.

Implied multipliers are provided for the two key variables of Walk and Wait. The convention of valuing these at twice IVT, now over 50-years old, is still used today in many contexts, including appraisal guidelines as set out in Table 1. If the multipliers are based upon older evidence and RP data (S5, S6, S13 and S14), which would have been the basis of the convention, the historic multiplier of 2 for Walk and Wait would indeed seem justified. Some commentators have questioned whether Walk and Wait should still be valued at twice IVT, with Table 1 showing that several countries recommend values below 2, and our results offer some support for lower multipliers.

Walk and Wait multipliers have gone down over time, presumably due to the increased opportunities to use time while walking or waiting and to the improvements in safety and walking/waiting conditions more widely. Retaining the RP approach as preferred but allowing for the reduction in multipliers over time (S1, S2, S9 and S10), there would seem to be justification for lower Walk multipliers, say around 1.75, but not for lower Wait multipliers. If, however, the results of the more widely used SP methods are preferred and combined with the more recent years (S3, S4, S11 and S12) then there would be strong support for moving to a figure around 1.5.

As for Headway, it is the one attribute where the SP data provides larger multipliers. In this context, it might be claimed that the SP evidence is more robust since the specification ofservice headway in such models will be more accurate than in RP models. However, a limitation of our results is that the unit Headway valuation might be expected to vary with the level ofservice frequency, with travellers valuing a given improvement to an hourly service more than to a service every half hour or 15 min, but the literature often does not examine such non-linearities and we have no direct empirical evidence on it. Moreover, some of the official recommendations in Table 1 exhibit a diminishing multiplier as the service headway increases due to moving from random arrivals and wait time to planned arrivals and headway penalties. For this reason, the Headway multipliers here might best serve a benchmarking purpose, with the lower multipliers for inter-urban trips (S19 and S22) being useful in this regard, and as such they provide an encouraging degree of support for central recommendations of the official guidance set out in Table 1.

Carjourney time spent in congested conditions is not routinely considered in transport appraisal. However, there is now a large and convincing body of evidence that a premium is attached to such time. No intertemporal or data type effects were detected. For Cong-nd, the multiplier is 1.33 within major metropolises (S25) and 1.51 elsewhere (S26). Distinguishing Cong-mod and Cong-hvy provides credible variations around these figures, with moderate and heavy congestion multipliers of 1.28 and 1.84 for major metropolises and 1.46 and 2.10 elsewhere. The official guidance for the congestion multiplier in Table 1 is supported by the findings here, albeit with the figures for Singapore for light and heavy congestion on the low side.

The other attribute for which there are no intertemporal or data type effects is displacement time. Here there is relatively little variation in implied multipliers, with a figure in the range 0.3 to 0.4 relevant to most travellers and very little difference between earlier and later departures. These tend to support the only official recommendations, of which we are aware, for Australia.

The remaining attributes relate to travel time variability. This is a contentious issue and is likely to lead to biased responses in SP exercises, and our dataset contains a few very large multipliers for MeanLate-Distn and MeanLate-X/Y. However, the RP based evidence implies even larger multipliers. As a result, it is unclear which set of numbers should be preferred, but what is clear is that the multipliers are large and that it cannot be argued that they are large simply because they are based on SP evidence. Taken overall, the evidence broadly supports official recommendations, but lower multipliers for inter-urban trips and higher multipliers for train and public transport users not apparent in the official recommendations would seem to be warranted.

As for the RR multipliers, they are lower for more recent data, presumably on the grounds that unreliability has become more expected and accepted, and these figures are preferred. They are also lower for inter-urban trips. The variation in the implied RR

## 6. Conclusions

This study has reported the most extensive review and meta-analysis of time multipliers ever made, spanning many years and countries. A wide range of important transport planning and appraisal-relevant multipliers has been covered: walk, wait, access/ egress, out-of-vehicle and connection time; public transport service headway; parking search time; time spent in congested traffic, with distinctions according to moderate and heavy congestion; travel time variability, covering lateness relative to timetable or expectation, schedule delay early, schedule delay late and the standard deviation of travel time (reliability ratio); and displacement time, including earlier and later departure time shifts.

The mean multipliers are credible and, where relationships exist between them, the results are broadly consistent. Walk, wait, access/egress, out-of-vehicle and connection time tend to have similar multipliers, which seems reasonable, whilst the premium attached to congested time seems sensible and the ordering ofthe moderate congestion, congestion and heavy congestion multipliers is consistent. Late arrival time is particularly highly valued with headway and displacement time having multipliers less than one as would be expected.

The impact of a wide range of variables on the multipliers has been investigated. Of particular interest are the findings that many multipliers have fallen over time and many multipliers depend upon the data used in estimation.

Estimated reductions in the walk, wait, access/egress, out-of-vehicle and connection time multipliers over time are attributed to advances in the quantity and quality of ICT which transform what was previously largely dead time into time where worthwhile activities can be undertaken. The reduction in the reliability ratio over time presumably stems from greater acceptance of unreliability in what might be perceived to be an increasingly unreliable transport world, along with greater opportunities for mitigation due to the increased availability of real-time information.

As for data type, RP based multipliers are larger for walk, wait, access/egress, out-of-vehicle and connection time and also a composite term covering mean lateness, schedule delay late and the reliability ratio. With regard to the various travel time terms that are not in-vehicle time, it is convincing that all five were found to have larger multipliers when obtained from RP data. This could be attributed to the artificial and sometimes complex nature of SP exercises and respondents’ primary focus may have been on cost and IVTwhich will lead to lower multipliers for other attributes to the extent that they might not have been given as much attention. These effects might be exacerbated by unrealistic attribute variation or poor explanation of that variation in SP exercises. It is, however, unclear why the RP multiplier for the composite late time is larger. But on the other hand, SP methods are now the default for esti mating valuations of travel time attributes.

The exception to the above pattern of results was for the headway multiplier where SP based valuations are larger and this could stem from the poor specification of service headway in RP models or the use of headway when in fact it is wait time that underpins choices.

A few other influences were apparent from mode used, journey purpose and distance. Perhaps reassuringly, the only effect from World Bank country income classification was for service headway and we did not discern any country specific effects. The investigation of quality related aspects of observations and studies did not raise any concerning issues.

The number of modifiers estimated to the multipliers is limited. A contributory factor is that some segmentation variables might have impacted the numerator and denominator elements of the multiplier terms similarly and hence will tend to cancel out or make it more difficult to recover significant effects. Indeed, we would expect far less variation in time multipliers than in the monetary val uations of the attributes here covered. Whilst the impacts of specific modifier variables tend to be relatively minor, quite large variations in multipliers can be apparent as a result of combining various individual modifier effects when, as reported in Table 8, the meta-model is applied to estimate implied multipliers across a range of scenarios.

The estimated meta-model implies plausible multipliers and can therefore serve a useful purpose in providing multipliers where none exist or benchmarking existing or emerging evidence. When compared to the official multiplier guidance that we have identified, the following conclusions can be drawn:

• There has been speculation that the long-established convention of valuing walk and wait time at twice IVT may no longer be valid, and the results presented here would provide some support based on detected reductions in walk and wait multipliers over time. A figure of 1.75 for the walk time multiplier would seem justified if the RP basis of the conventional wisdom was retained. However, if SP results are regarded to be more reliable then a reduction to 1.50 for both walk and wait time multipliers is warranted, supporting the multipliers of 1.50 that are recommended by some administrations.

• The headway multipliers implied by the meta-model serve a useful benchmarking purpose and broadly support those recommended in official guidance.

• The displacement time multipliers could be used to significantly enhance existing official guidance which only exists for Australia.

• Official congestion multiplier guidance is generally supported by the findings here, but we would recommend adoption of the implied multipliers and particularly the distinction between moderate and heavy congestion.

• The results broadly support the large official late time multipliers, with additional distinctions between urban and inter-urban trips and between rail and public transport users and users of other modes warranted.

• Official guidance exhibits a very large range ofreliability ratio multipliers, and the extreme valuations would seem to be difficult to justify. It is recommended that the meta-model’s results are adopted; a range of around 0.6 to 0.9 would seem appropriate and within that lower valuations for inter-urban trips would seem sensible.

• The results do not support the widely used Cost Savings Approach to valuing all aspects of time the same for business travellers. This finding might challenge the legitimacy of the Cost Savings Approach.
